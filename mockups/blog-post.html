What we learned deploying Hermes in a 12-seat noodle shop.

Six months in, here's the operating manual we wish we'd had — including the embarrassing parts.

By Eric Leung · 12 min read · Filed under FIELD NOTES
CONTENTS

  2. Week one
  3. The incident
  4. What worked
  5. Operating manual
  6. What we'd do differently

Mama Kee's Kitchen, Whampoa. Twelve seats, a stainless-steel counter, and a WhatsApp business account. We'd been hired to deploy a Cantonese-speaking agent for take-away orders. Here's how it actually went.

01 · The brief.

Wing, the owner, was losing two hours a day to phone orders. She'd tried a chatbot widget from a SaaS company six months earlier. It spoke English only, didn't understand Cantonese addresses, and the demo promised things it couldn't deliver. When it broke, the support line didn't answer.

Our brief: replace phone orders during peak (5:30pm–10pm), answer FAQs in Cantonese, send an FPS payment link, hand off to a human for anything outside the menu. Nothing more.

02 · Week one.

Day one, we connected the existing Telegram Bot. Day two, we built a small menu JSON. Day three, we ran the first 50 conversations in shadow mode (Hermes suggested; Wing confirmed). Day four, Wing sent Hermes live, on her own phone, with me on the other end of a WeChat thread.

The first 30 conversations went fine. The 31st was a customer asking if the shrimp dumpling contained peanut oil. The model said "I'm not sure." Wing typed back "yes it does." That's where it sat, for two weeks.

"I don't need an AI that knows everything. I need an AI that knows what it doesn't know, and asks me."

03 · The incident.

Three weeks in, a customer ordered 4 portions of star-fried rice at 9:47pm. Kitchen had already closed at 9:30. Hermes said "Sure, will be ready in 20 minutes." The customer arrived at 10:15pm. Wing had to comp the meal.

We added a hard check: between 9:30pm and 5:30pm, Hermes responds "Kitchen closed — opening again tomorrow at 5:30pm." Embarrassing. Obvious. Necessary.

The fix

Two lines of prompt, a single cron-style time check, and a "kitchen closed" template. Now whenever anyone asks for an order outside hours, Hermes responds correctly, every time. We've shipped this pattern to every deployment since.

04 · What worked.

  • Handoff triggers. Hermes never tries to handle a refund, a complaint, or an allergy question. It forwards, with full context.
  • Daily review. Wing spends 5 minutes each morning reading the previous night's transcripts. We get a Slack digest of any flagged conversation.
  • Menu as code. When Wing changes prices, she edits a Google Sheet. We sync it every 15 minutes. No deploy.
  • FPS links, not Stripe. HK customers pay by FPS or PayMe; Hermes sends an FPS deep link. 80% conversion vs ~20% with Stripe links.

05 · The operating manual.

After six months, here's what we tell every restaurant owner we onboard:

  1. Don't promise the agent can do things it can't. Customers remember.
  2. The handoff threshold is more important than the prompt. Get that wrong, and the agent becomes a liability.
  3. Read the transcripts every day for the first month. After that, weekly. Build the habit.
  4. Have a clear escalation channel to a human. SMS, WhatsApp, phone — whatever the owner actually answers.
  5. Review the menu with the model every time you change the menu. The model doesn't know anything; it reads.

06 · What we'd do differently.

Start with a smaller menu. We tried to onboard all 47 dishes at once. It would have been better to start with the 12 most-ordered items, get comfortable, then expand.

Skip Telegram initially. WhatsApp-only would have been simpler for Wing's customer base.

Have a printed PDF of the operating manual for the owner, not just a Google Doc. Wing prints things. We now print things.
